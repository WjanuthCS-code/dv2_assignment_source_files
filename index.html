A data story about Australian frogs

The Fading Chorus

Australia is home to more than 240 kinds of frog, and almost all of them live nowhere else on Earth. Since the 1980s, many have quietly disappeared from the creeks and swamps where they once called. This page follows the data: who is declining, who is bouncing back, and when frogs sing.

01

Meet Australia's frogs

Australian frogs belong to a handful of families. Tree frogs and the ground-dwelling "froglets" make up most of the species we hear. Each box below is one species: switch between counting species and counting recordings to see how a few common frogs dominate what people hear, while threatened species (coloured) are rarely heard at all.

Only 24 of the 53 nationally threatened frogs appear in FrogID's public records. The others are extinct, too rare to be recorded, or have their records withheld to protect them.

02

A silent crisis

Scientists have counted threatened frogs at hundreds of sites for decades. Combined into a single index, the numbers tell a stark story: on average, threatened frog populations are now around 3% of what they were in 1985. A major cause is chytrid, a fungal disease that attacks frogs' skin, which they use to breathe and drink.

03

Who is declining, who is recovering?

Not every threatened frog is losing ground. Comparing the first and last count at each monitoring site shows some species are recovering while others continue to slide.

Reading the map

Scientists survey threatened frogs at hundreds of sites, mostly in the south-east where the biggest programs run. Large circles in Victoria and the Snowy Mountains are long-running programs for the Southern Bell Frog and the corroboree frogs.

Red regions are where most sites now have fewer frogs than when monitoring began; blue regions are where most have more. Site locations are shown only by region to protect the frogs.

Some of the most endangered frogs have the thinnest records. With so few sites counted in recent years, we know less about how they are doing today. That is why citizen science matters.

04

When frogs breed

Male frogs call to attract a mate, so a frog call is a sign of breeding. Recordings from FrogID show each species has its own season.

05

The invader

Cane Toads were released in Queensland in 1935 to control beetles in sugarcane. They are poisonous to many native animals and now spread across northern Australia.

Keep the chorus going

Every recording counts. FrogID's 1.18 million calls were collected by ordinary Australians with a phone. Recording frogs near you helps scientists track where species are holding on, and where they are disappearing.
