A data story about Australian frogs

The Fading Chorus

Australia is home to about 240 known native frog species.1 Since the 1980s, many have quietly disappeared from the creeks and swamps where they once called. This page follows the data: who is declining, who is bouncing back, and when frogs sing.

01

Meet Australia's frogs

Australian frogs belong to a handful of families. Tree frogs and the ground-dwelling froglets and toadlets make up most of the species that citizen scientists record. Each box below is one family, split by how many of its species are nationally threatened. Most of the threatened species are froglets, toadlets or tree frogs.

Threatened frogs are also rarely heard: the most-recorded threatened species, the Endangered Sloane's Froglet, has 2,777 FrogID recordings, compared with almost 220,000 for the Common Eastern Froglet. Fewer than half of the 53 nationally listed frogs appear in FrogID's public records at all.

02

A silent crisis

Scientists have counted threatened frogs at hundreds of sites for decades. Combined into a single index, the numbers tell a stark story: on average, threatened frog populations are now around 3% of what they were in 1985. A major cause is chytrid,2 a fungal disease that attacks frogs' skin, which they use to breathe and drink.

03

Who is declining, who is recovering?

Not every threatened frog is losing ground. Comparing the first and latest count at each monitoring site shows that some species are recovering, while others have fallen at most of the sites where they are watched.

Reading the map

Scientists survey threatened frogs at hundreds of sites, mostly in the south-east where the biggest programs run. The largest circles are long-running programs: the Victorian Volcanic Plain (Southern Bell Frog), Bondo in NSW (Northern Corroboree Frog) and the Hunter region (Green and Golden Bell Frog).

Red regions are where most sites now have fewer frogs than when monitoring began; blue regions are where most have more. Site locations are shown only by region to protect the frogs.

Some of the most endangered frogs have the thinnest records. With so few sites counted in recent years, we know less about how they are doing today. That is why citizen science matters.

04

When frogs breed

Male frogs call to attract a mate, so a frog call is a sign of breeding. FrogID recordings of eight featured species show that most call loudest from October to January, though a few, like the Common Mist Frog, call almost all year.

05

The invader

Cane Toads were released near Cairns, Queensland, in 1935 to control beetles in sugarcane.3 Their poison can kill native predators, and they have spread across northern Australia as far as the Kimberley.

Why toads matter for frogs

The poison Cane Toads produce can kill many native predators, and some predator populations have declined as toads spread.3

Most recordings come from south-east Queensland, where many people live and record frogs. Dots across the Top End and the Kimberley show how far the toad has spread from where it was released.

More dots in a place can mean more toads, or simply more people recording, so this map shows where toads are heard, not exactly how many there are.

Keep the chorus going

Every recording counts. FrogID's 1.18 million calls were collected by ordinary Australians with a phone. Recording frogs near you helps scientists track where species are holding on, and where they are disappearing.
